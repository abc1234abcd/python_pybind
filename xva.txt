counterparty risk: also called credit risk or default risk, refers to the possibility that the other party in a financial contract(e.g derivative, loan, or bond) fails to meet its obligations.
counterparty risk modelling quantifies this risk to help banks, hedge funds, and corporations ***manage exposures.***

e.g. borrower will not repay a loan, a hedge fund it trades derivatives with goes bankrupt.

key components of counterparty risk modelling:

a. probability default: estimate the likelihood of counterparty default within a time horizon. e.g. credit scoring models: altman z-score, merton structural model. statistical models: logistic regression, machine learning. market based models: CDS spread, bond yields.
    in Basel III requirement: banks must estiamte PD based on historical data, borrower creditworthiness, and economic conditions.
b. loss given default: the % of exposure loss(EAD), accounting for recoveries. formula: LGD = 1 - recovery rate. recovery rate depends on collateral, seniority, and bankruptcy laws.
c. exposure at default: the amount at risk(loss amount) when default occurs. For loan, outstanding principal + accreued interest. For derivatives: potential future exposure. 
d. credit valuation adjustment(CVA): adjust the value of a derivative to account for counterparty risk. CVA = expected exposure * Probability Default*Loss Given Default. banks use CVA pricing to charge for risk in OTC derivatives.
e. wrong way risk. when exposure to a counterparty increases as its credit quality worsen. aka, links exposure and credit quality. Adjust PD/EAD correlation.

manage exposures refers to the process of identifying, assessing, and controlling risks in various cnotexts, such as finance. The goal is to minimize potential harm or loss by taking proactive measures.
e.g risk management, insurance and liability, workplace safety, etc.

Basel III uses PD, LGD, EAD to calculate capital: risk-weighted assets(RWA), which determine the minimum capital requirement(typically 8% of  RWA under Basel III):

e.g.   RWA = EAD * risk weight(where risk weight depends on PD, LGD, and maturity).
step 1: calculate expected loss: EL = PD*LGD*EAD


common counterparty risk models:
a. merton model: uses equity volatility to estimate default probability.
b. creditMatrics(JPMorgan): simulates credit migrations based on ratings.
c. KMV models(Moody's): estimates distrance to default using stock prices.
d. Monte Carlo simulations: simulates future exposures in derivatives.
e. ISDA SIMM: standardized initial margin model fo runcleared swaps.

how banks and hedge funds use counterparty risk models:
a. risk limits: setting max exposure per counterparty.
b. collateral management: requiring margin. e.g CSA agreements.
c. pricing adjustment: charging CVA/DVA(debit valuation adjustment).
d. stress testing: assessing risk under extreme scenarios.
e. regulatory compliance: basel III, Dodd-Frank, and EMIR require capital for counterparty risk.

exotic and structured derivatives: are complex financial instrucments customized to meet specific risk return objectives. Unlike vanila derivatives, they have non-stand payoff structure, embedded leverage, or conditional triggers.

exotic derivatives: 
a. barrier options: payout depends on whether the underlying asset hits a predefined price level. e.g a knock-out put on Apple. if Apple crashes before bankruptcy, the option payout may go unpaid.
b. basket options: Option on a portfolio of assets(e.g stocks, currency)
c. lookback options: holder can 'look back' and choose the optimal price for payoff calculations.

why use exotics? tailored risk exposure(e.g cheaper than vanilla options if barriers are set.) Hedging unusal risk(e.g currency crashes, commodity price spikes), speculative leverage(e.g binaries for high-risk bets.)


structured solutions: are customized instruments designed to meet specific investment, risk management, or financing needs by combining traditional securities(e.g bonds, equity) with derivatives(options, swaps).
they are tailored to optimize risk-return profits, tax efficiency, or regulatory capital.

structured derivatives/: combine multiple instrucments(options, swaps, bonds) into a single product, often linked to market performance.
a. CPPI structures: dynamic portfolio insurance(bond + leveraged derivatives.)
b. credit linked notes(CLNs): bond + credit default swap(inverstor takes credit risk)
c. autocallables: bond + option; early redemption if the underlying hits a trigger.
d. structured deposits: bank deposits with returns linked to amrket indices or derivatives.

key mechanics:
a. underlying assets: derives value from equities, rates, credit, commodities, or FX.
b. derivative component: options, swaps, or triggers modify payouts(e.g. autocallable features).
c. payout structure: define returns(eg. fixed coupon + conditional upside.)

real work example: hedge FX exposure with structured forwards.

X-Value adjustment: a collective term for a suite of adjustments applied to the valuation of derivative and financial instrucments to account for credit risk, funding costs, capital requirements, and other real world frictions.
It ensures that pricing relects true economic costs beyond the idealized 'risk-free' assumption.
e.g CVA: credit valuation adjustment, DVA: debit valuation adjustment, FVA: funding valation adjustment, Capital valuation adjustment(reflects the cost of holding regulatory capital Basel III.)
MVA: margin valuation adjustment(covers intial margin costs in cleared trades.)


why XVA matters: traditional pricing derivatives are using 'risk-free' rates, ignoring real-world costs. real world complexity: counterparty can default(CVA). collateral and funding have costs(FVA). regulatoin require capital reserves(KVA)
